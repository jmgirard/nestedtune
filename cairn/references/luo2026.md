## Terminology note

**This paper contains no nested cross-validation at all.** It is the theory
underneath the distinction that IP3 asserts, and it names the two sides
crisply. For an algorithm 𝒜, a distribution P, a training size n, and a fitted
model f̂ₙ = 𝒜(𝒟ₙ):

- **Model risk** R_P(f̂ₙ) = E[ℓ(f̂ₙ(X), Y) | f̂ₙ] — random, conditional on the
  particular training draw. The question **EvaluateModel**.
- **Algorithm risk** R_{P,n}(𝒜) = E[R_P(f̂ₙ)] — averaged over training draws
  too. The question **EvaluateAlg**.

These are `bates2023.md`'s Err_XY and Err, and `austern2020.md`'s two
estimands, under a third pair of names. The paper's opening complaint is that
practice conflates them: a claim that some method performs well on a problem is
an EvaluateAlg claim, routinely justified by a holdout score, which answers
EvaluateModel (p. 2428).

**Black-box test** (Definition 2.1, p. 2432) is the paper's other key object:
any procedure allowed to call 𝒜 on adaptively chosen datasets as many times as
it likes, but not to read 𝒜's implementation. Cross-validation, nested
cross-validation, bootstrapping, and every resampling scheme on this shelf are
black-box tests. That is what makes the results below binding rather than
academic.

## What it establishes

**Result 1 (Theorem 3.1, p. 2434) — the hardness result.** Let the loss take
values in [0, B], let T̂ be any black-box test that is *assumption-free valid*
— Type I error ≤ α for **every** algorithm and **every** distribution with
R_{P,n}(𝒜) ≥ τ — and set τ̃ = τ(1 + (α⁻¹−1)/(N−1)). Then for any 𝒜, P with
R_{P,n}(𝒜) < τ,

> power ≤ α · [1 + (τ̃ − R_{P,n}(𝒜)) / (R_P^max − τ̃)]^(N/n) ∧ 1

where R_P^max is the largest risk any function can have under P. **The exponent
is N/n.** If the data available (N) is not many times the evaluation sample
size (n), the power is a small constant multiple of α — no better than random
for small α, no matter how clever the test.

**Result 2 — EvaluateModel is easy in the same setting.** With a holdout of
size m, the empirical mean loss estimates R_P(f̂ₙ) to O_P(1/√m) by
concentration, and the test is immediate (p. 2435). The hardness is entirely in
averaging over training draws, not in estimating a risk.

**Result 3 (Theorem 3.6, p. 2437) — a trivial test is nearly optimal.** Split
the data into ⌊N/(n+1)⌋ disjoint batches; train on n points of each batch;
score the one held-out point; compare the sum against a Binomial. Its power is
exactly α(1 + (τ − R_{P,n}(𝒜))/(1 − τ))^⌊N/(n+1)⌋ ∧ 1, which for large N, n and
R_P^max ≈ 1 nearly attains the Theorem 3.1 bound. The paper notes how
counter-intuitive this is: the naive split wastes almost all the data, and is
still near-optimal, because the problem itself is hard.

**Result 4 (Theorem 3.7, p. 2438) — unbounded loss is worse.** With an
unbounded loss, no assumption-free valid black-box test can exceed power α at
all — strictly no better than random. A referee also pointed out (acknowledged
on p. 2438) that with a non-atomic marginal on X, even EvaluateModel becomes
impossible under unbounded loss.

**Result 5 (Theorem 4.3, p. 2440) — algorithmic stability does not help, except
where it makes the question vanish.** Define ℓ_q-stability β_q(𝒜, P, n) as the
worst-case q-norm change in the loss at a fresh test point when one training
point is dropped (Definition 4.1). Two regimes:

- **Consistency regime**, β₁ = o(n⁻¹) or β₂ = o(n^{−1/2}). Proposition 4.2
  gives E|R_P(f̂ₙ) − R_{P,n}(𝒜)| ≤ 2n·β₁ and the ℓ₂ analogue ≤ √n·β₂, so the
  two risks coincide asymptotically and EvaluateAlg reduces to the easy
  question.
- **Everything else**, γ_q ≳ n^{−1/q}. Theorem 4.3 reproduces the Theorem 3.1
  bound *verbatim*.

There is no intermediate regime — a phase transition, not a gradient. The paper
draws the direct corollary that the o(n^{−1/2}) stability `austern2020.md`
assumes is **necessary in general**, not merely convenient.

**Result 6 (footnote 3, p. 2439) — a sharp note on `bayle2020.md`.** The
*loss stability* Bayle et al. use is strictly weaker than the ℓ₂-stability
above and is in general **not sufficient** for Proposition 4.2's consistency
conclusion. This is a precise external qualification of a source already on
this shelf.

**Result 7 (Section 5) — comparison is equally hard.** Theorem 5.2 bounds the
power of any assumption-free valid test of CompareAlg by the same
(·)^{N/n} shape; Theorems 5.3 and 5.4 show stability again fails to help
outside the consistency regime. The paper states, without giving details, that
a Binomial-style construction shows the comparison bound is approximately tight
too (p. 2445).

**Result 8 — the inference–prediction tradeoff.** For fixed N, power for
EvaluateAlg needs n ≪ N; but a small n means 𝒜 returns worse models, so
R_{P,n}(𝒜) is itself higher and less relevant to what you would deploy
(p. 2435). Attributed to Rinaldo, Wasserman & G'Sell (2019).

## Extracted values

The paper is theoretical; it reports no simulations and no tables. The one
concrete instance it works out (Section 3.2.1, p. 2437):

Binary Y with a perfect oracle classifier, P(Y = 0) = P(Y = 1) = 0.5, τ = 0.5
(the hypothesis being simply "better than chance"), and N = n + 1. Then

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Power of the Binomial test | (2 − 2·R_{P,n}(𝒜))·α, so **≤ 2α** |
| Upper bound for *any* black-box test | ≈ (2 − 2·R_{P,n}(𝒜))^{1+1/n}·α, again **≈ 2α** |

Stated conditions carried throughout: |𝒳| = ∞ or |𝒴| = ∞ (Remark 3.4 explains
why — with a finite space and no computational budget, exhaustive search
characterizes 𝒜 completely); loss in [0, B]; and Remark 3.2 notes the bound is
scale-invariant in B.

The stability threshold in Theorem 4.3 requires γ_q ≥ 2B/n^{1/q}.

## Bearing on nestedtune

- **This is the deepest available justification for IP3, and it is stronger
  than "the estimate describes the procedure".** The two quantities are not
  merely different; one is easy and one is provably hard. A user who reads a
  nested estimate as though it were a statement about their fitted model is not
  making a philosophical error, they are crossing the line between a tractable
  inference problem and an intractable one.
- **It is the sharpest possible support for GP5, and it changes what the G6 row
  should say.** `tidymodels-nested-cv-gaps.md` currently records G6's three
  blockers as loss stability being unestablished, the valid interval targeting
  a different estimand, and the per-observation losses being discarded. This
  paper adds a fourth that is *categorical rather than practical*: for
  v-fold nesting, n = N(1 − 1/v), so **N/n = v/(v−1)** — about 1.11 at v = 10,
  1.25 at v = 5. That is exactly the N ≈ n regime where Theorem 3.1 says no
  assumption-free valid test can have power meaningfully above α. It is not
  that nobody has built the interval yet; it is that an assumption-free one
  cannot exist at the sample ratio nested CV operates at.
- **The hardness is about *inference*, not about point estimation, and the
  distinction must not be blurred.** Nothing here says the nested point
  estimate of R_{P,n}(𝒜) is wrong, biased, or useless — `varma2006.md` and
  `tsamardinos2018.md` both measure it as approximately unbiased. What is
  impossible is a universally valid test or confidence interval for it at
  N ≈ n. The package computes a point estimate and declines to ship an
  interval; that is precisely the line this theorem draws.
- **It bounds what a stability assumption could ever buy.** If G6 is ever
  revisited, Result 5 says the only escape is the consistency regime
  β₂ = o(n^{−1/2}) — and in that regime the algorithm risk collapses onto the
  model risk, so the interval one gets is not really an interval for the
  nested estimand. Establishing loss stability for a *tuned workflow* was
  already an open blocker; Result 6 says even establishing it would not be
  enough, since loss stability is the weaker notion.
- **A caution about how to phrase this in documentation.** The paper's own
  advice (p. 2435) is that with limited data, risk claims should be read as
  claims about the fitted model rather than the algorithm. That is *not*
  licence to relabel a nestedtune estimate as the final model's score — the
  f̂ₙ in question is trained on n = analysis-set rows, not on all N, so it is
  neither the algorithm risk nor the shipped model's risk. IP3 stands; this
  paper explains why the honest alternative is a holdout, not a reinterpretation
  of the nested number.
- **Result 3 is uncomfortable and should be recorded as such.** A trivial
  disjoint-batch split is nearly power-optimal for EvaluateAlg. For the
  *inference* question, elaborate resampling buys almost nothing over sample
  splitting. This does not touch the estimation question the package actually
  serves, but any future claim that nesting improves *inference* is now
  refutable from this shelf.
- **Nothing here bears on IP1, IP2, IP4 or the orchestration gaps G1–G5.** The
  paper has no opinion on leakage, reproducibility, or ecosystem plumbing.

## Oracle status

**No oracle, and none possible from this source.**

The results are upper bounds on the power of hypothetical tests. There is no
statistic to compute, no simulation to replicate, and no worked numeric example
beyond the 2α illustration in Section 3.2.1 — which is an algebraic
substitution into Theorem 3.6, not a measurement. GP2 type (3), analytic
results, requires a value the implementation must reproduce; this paper
supplies constraints on what any implementation could achieve, which is a
different and non-testable thing.

Its practical role is as a **negative oracle for planning**: it rules out a
class of features rather than validating one.

## Open questions

- Whether the N/n bound is the binding constraint for repeated or Monte-Carlo
  outer schemes, where the effective ratio differs. The paper fixes a single
  n and N and never treats repeated resampling — observed 2026-07-31.
- Whether the proofs' counterexample constructions require pathological
  algorithms that no tidymodels workflow could instantiate. The constructions
  are in the unread Supplementary Material, and Remark 3.4's infinite-cardinality
  assumption suggests they may be delicate — observed 2026-07-31.
- What "computational budget" versions of these bounds look like for finite
  spaces. Remark 3.4 explicitly leaves this to future work and points at Luo &
  Barber (2024) for partial progress; that paper is not on the shelf —
  observed 2026-07-31.
- Whether conformal risk control or distribution-free excess-risk control
  (both named in the Discussion, p. 2448) offer a route to a shippable interval
  under a relaxed target. Neither is developed here, and neither source is on
  the shelf — observed 2026-07-31.
